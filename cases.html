The Evidentiary Standard Has Changed

Four Jurisdictions. One Pattern.

Courts across the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada have established that the absence of a contemporaneous verification record is treated as evidence of fault — independent of whether an error was made. These are the cases that established the precedent.

The Cases

Miller v. Regions Bank · N.D. Alabama · Case No. 2024-cv-01324 · 2026
The Two-Track Doctrine
AI hallucinations in a federal brief. Judge Mooty applied a two-track sanctions doctrine: the error itself — submitting a brief containing fabricated citations generated by ChatGPT — received a public reprimand. But the destruction of the ChatGPT account after the court's order — eliminating all contemporaneous record of what occurred and when — received adverse inference, contempt findings, and a six-month suspension from practice.
The distinction was explicit: the AI error was a professional mistake. The destruction of the record was evidence of fault. The absence of a contemporaneous record was converted into proof that the attorney had something to hide.
The sanction was for the missing record, not the error. Six-month suspension. Source: PACER.
H.C. v. Contreras · California 5th DCA · F089316 · 2026
Three Gates. Zero Records.
A fabricated citation was flagged by opposing counsel during the proceedings. The trial judge adopted the citation anyway, copying it verbatim into the ruling — including an identical typographical error that proved it came from the same AI-generated source. Three separate verification gates — the drafting attorney, the reviewing attorney, and the judge — all failed. No contemporaneous record of review existed at any stage.
The appellate court identified the pattern through the typographical error. The case was not merely reversed — it was reassigned to a different judge entirely. The pattern of failure was structural: each actor assumed someone upstream had already verified.
Reversed and reassigned to a new judge. Source: courts.ca.gov.
CPS / Tobosaru · EWHC 1720 (Admin) · United Kingdom · 2026
The State as Actor.
The Crown Prosecution Service — the state prosecution authority of England and Wales — submitted hallucinated case citations to the High Court in an extradition appeal. The fabricated material migrated from one internal CPS document to another, surviving the permission stage unchallenged. Each reader in the chain assumed that someone upstream had already verified the citations. No one had.
This case extended the pattern beyond private practice: the state itself was the actor. The CPS subsequently admitted "human error in failure to verify." The case demonstrated that the verification gap is not limited to individual lawyers or small firms — it is structural.
CPS admitted: "human error in failure to verify." Source: BAILII.
Law Society of Ontario · Mary Hyun-Sook Lee · Canada · 2026
First Canadian Suspension for AI Misconduct.
A lawyer submitted ChatGPT-fabricated citations in an estate matter before the Ontario courts. When questioned directly by the judge about whether AI had been used, the lawyer denied it. The Law Society Tribunal was explicit in its reasoning: the dishonesty — the absence of any truthful contemporaneous account of what had occurred — was what drove the severity of the sanction.
This is believed to be the first time a Canadian legal regulator has suspended a lawyer specifically for AI-related misconduct. The case established that the pattern documented in US courts applies cross-jurisdictionally. Criminal contempt proceedings have been separately referred to the Ontario Attorney General.
6-month suspension · $10,000 costs · Criminal contempt referral to Attorney General. Source: Law Society of Ontario.

The Regulatory Context

USA · 2024
ABA Formal Opinion 512
The American Bar Association established that lawyers using generative AI must apply the same competence (Rule 1.1) and supervision (Rule 5.1) standards as for any other tool. Every AI-assisted filing must be independently verified before submission to a court or reliance in client matters. The Opinion did not create a new obligation — it clarified that existing obligations apply to AI outputs.

USA · 2026
Rhode Island Rule 1.1 Amendment
The Rhode Island Supreme Court amended the competence rule to explicitly include understanding the benefits and risks of generative AI. This is among the first US states to formally codify AI understanding as a component of professional competence, alongside Interim Guidelines covering both lawyers and judicial officers.
ZTR documents compliance with the verification requirement.
EU · 2026
EU AI Act · Article 17
The EU AI Act requires documented quality management systems for high-risk AI systems, with human oversight built into workflows and demonstrable on demand. The EN 18286 harmonised standard — the QMS framework for Article 17 compliance — received formal vote in June 2026 and publication is imminent. The standard defines what a QMS must contain but cannot prove that the QMS was actually followed.
ZTR provides the contemporaneous record that oversight was exercised — the demonstrability layer that the standard requires but cannot itself provide.
UK · 2026
UKJT Legal Statement on AI
The UK Jurisdiction Taskforce established a dual standard: professionals may be negligent for failing to use AI where a competent colleague would, and they may be negligent for using it without adequate verification. The standard moves with adoption — as AI becomes the norm, the duty to verify AI outputs becomes part of the baseline standard of care.
ZTR documents that the verification standard was met, case by case, document by document.
UK · 2026
Nuffield Foundation · Sargeant Policy Brief
Dr. Holli Sargeant's July 2026 Nuffield Foundation Policy Brief documented that of 45 AI tools deployed in the England and Wales justice system, only 7 (15.5%) have been independently evaluated. Human oversight is treated as the principal safeguard in UK deployment policy — yet no deployment evaluation examines whether human oversight operates effectively in practice. The gap between assuming oversight and demonstrating oversight is the structural condition ZTR addresses.

CROSS-JURISDICTIONAL · 2026
The Sanctions Doctrine
Across all documented cases, a consistent pattern has emerged: courts and regulators penalise the absence of a contemporaneous verification record more severely than the underlying error. The AI hallucination receives a reprimand. The missing record — the inability to demonstrate that verification occurred — receives contempt, suspension, or reversal. The missing record has become the sanctionable fact.
